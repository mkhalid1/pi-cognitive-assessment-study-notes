# Abstract reasoning

**In one line:** puzzles made of shapes, arrows and patterns, with no words or numbers to lean on, where you spot the rule and apply it.

Abstract reasoning is the third content area of the PI Cognitive Assessment. You can meet up to 16 abstract questions in one test. For many people this is the least familiar area, and also the one that improves fastest with a little practice.

## What it measures

PI describes this area as the ability to identify patterns, think strategically and solve novel problems using logic. "Novel" is the key word. You cannot know the answer in advance. You have to work out a rule you have never seen, from a few examples, in a few seconds.

PI says its abstract items do not use Latin letters for patterns or rotations, so they work the same way in every language. Expect shapes, symbols, shading and direction.

## How the questions look

PI does not publish a list of abstract question types. The formats below are the ones we use for practice. They exercise the same skill of finding and applying a visual rule. Because these notes are plain text, the shapes are drawn with symbols. The real test uses images.

| Format | What you do |
|---|---|
| Series | A row of shapes changes step by step. Pick what comes next. |
| Grids (matrices) | A 3 by 3 grid follows rules across rows and down columns. Fill the gap. |
| Analogies | Shape A changes into shape B. Apply the same change to shape C. |
| Odd one out | Four shapes share a feature. Find the one that does not. |

## A method that works

1. **List what can change.** Shape, number, fill (solid or hollow), size, position, direction. Most items change only one or two of these.
2. **Compare neighbours, one feature at a time.** From the first to the second item, what changed? From the second to the third? Say it in words: "turns a quarter clockwise", "one more triangle", "dot moves one place right".
3. **Check the rule on every step.** A rule that fits two steps but not the third is the wrong rule.
4. **Predict before you look at the options.** Decide what the answer should look like, then find it. This stops attractive wrong options pulling you in.
5. **For grids, check rows and columns.** A common grid rule is "each shape once in every row and column". Another is "the third box combines the first two".
6. **For odd one out, look for a rule that picks out exactly one.** If your rule picks out two shapes, it is not the intended rule.

### Common rules to look for

| Rule | Example |
|---|---|
| Alternation | ◆ ◇ ◆ ◇ |
| Counting up or down | ★★★, ★★, ★ |
| Rotation by a fixed angle | ↑ ← ↓ (a quarter turn anticlockwise each time) |
| Fill changing step by step | □, a square half shaded, ■ |
| Movement along a row | a dot moving one place left each step |
| Each item once per row and column | a 3 by 3 grid using three different shapes |
| Two rules at once | the fill alternates while the shape turns |

## Traps that cost points

- **Spotting one change and missing a second.** If the count grows *and* the shape alternates, an option that gets only one right is a trap.
- **Clockwise versus anticlockwise.** Check the direction twice. Options often include the mirror image of the right answer.
- **Inventing a rule that is too clever.** The intended rule is usually the simplest one that fits every step. If you need three sentences to explain it, look again.
- **Choosing by look rather than by rule.** The option that "feels" balanced is not always the one the rule produces.
- **Sinking time into one puzzle.** Abstract items can swallow 40 seconds if you let them. If no rule appears in about 15 seconds, guess and move on. Only correct answers count.

## Timing

Simple series and odd-one-out items can take under 10 seconds once you have seen a few. Grids and two-rule items take longer. Because abstract items are the least like school work, the first few on test day can feel slow. Doing 30 or so beforehand, such as the ones below, removes most of that warm-up cost. See the [timing guide](../timing.md) for a full pacing plan.

## Practice items

These 5 items are original, written for these notes. They are not taken from the PI Cognitive Assessment or from PI's sample questions. Try them in **1 minute 10 seconds**, then open the explanations.

**Item 1.** What comes next?

```
↑   ↗   →   ↘   ?
```

A) ↓  B) ↙  C) ←  D) ↑

**Item 2.** What comes next?

```
▲     ■■     ▲▲▲     ?
```

A) ▲▲▲▲  B) ■■■  C) ■■■■  D) ▲■▲■

**Item 3.** Each row and each column uses each shape once. Which shape replaces the question mark?

```
○   □   △
□   △   ○
△   ○   ?
```

A) ○  B) △  C) □  D) ●

**Item 4.** ▲ is to ▲▲ as ● is to:
A) ●  B) ○○  C) ●●  D) ▲▲  E) ●●●

**Item 5.** Which is the odd one out?
A) ↑  B) →  C) ↓  D) ←  E) ↗

<details>
<summary>Answers and explanations, items 1 to 5</summary>

**Item 1: A) ↓.** The arrow turns 45 degrees clockwise each step: up, up-right, right, down-right, then down.

**Item 2: C) ■■■■.** Two rules: the count grows by one, and the shape alternates triangle, square, triangle, square. The fourth step is four squares. *Why not A:* right count, wrong shape.

**Item 3: C) □.** The bottom row has △ and ○, so it needs □. The third column has △ and ○ as well, which confirms it.

**Item 4: C) ●●.** The shape is doubled and keeps its fill. *Why not B:* two shapes is right, but the fill changed. *Why not D:* the triangles belong to the first pair; the change is applied to the circle.

**Item 5: E) ↗.** The other four point straight up, down, left or right. ↗ is the only diagonal.

</details>

## Score yourself

| Item | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Answer | A | C | C | C | E |

- **5:** abstract reasoning is a strength. Work on speed: you should be able to name most of these rules in under 8 seconds.
- **3 to 4:** look at which rule types you missed. Two-rule items (like item 2) and direction items (like item 1) are the usual culprits. Reread the "common rules" table.
- **2 or fewer:** go through the method again and redo the items untimed, saying each rule out loud in a short sentence. Then redo them against the clock.

Practice scores are a guide to where to work, not a prediction of your PI score.
