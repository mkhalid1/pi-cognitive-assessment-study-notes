# How PI Cognitive Assessment scoring works

Short version: your score depends only on how many questions you answer correctly. That number is converted to a scaled score between 100 and 450. The employer compares it with a target score set for the job. There is no universal pass mark.

Everything below comes from PI's own support articles, linked at the end.

## Number correct is all that counts

PI says scores are "solely based on the number of questions the participant answers correctly." It gives this example: one candidate answers 20 questions and gets 15 right; another answers 40 and also gets 15 right. They get the same score.

Two things follow:

1. **A wrong answer costs nothing.** PI does not take marks off for wrong answers, and the number you attempted is not part of the score. So never leave a question blank, and never let the last 20 seconds run out with items unanswered.
2. **Accuracy matters as much as speed.** Rushing through 45 questions with many slips is no better than a careful 35. The best score comes from the most correct answers, at whatever pace gets you there.

## The scaled score

The number of correct answers (your raw score) is converted to a scaled score between **100 and 450**. PI does not publish the conversion table.

PI uses the scaled score because raw scores are not always comparable. Different versions of the test, and the extended-time versions, are linked by statistical equating, so the same scaled score means the same thing whichever version you took. PI's example: a scaled score of 270 is always a good match for a job with a target of 270.

PI describes the scale in two ways:

- The score is **referenced against the general population**, whose average PI gives as **250**. The norm for a particular job may differ from that average.
- The assessment is **criterion-referenced**: the score is based on your own number of correct answers rather than on a ranking against other people taking it at the same time.

Both are PI's wording. In practice: your score reflects how many you got right, and 250 is PI's figure for the general population average.

## What the report shows

PI's report has three kinds of numbers:

| Number | What it is | Used for decisions? |
|---|---|---|
| Scaled score (top of the report) | Your score on the 100 to 450 scale | Yes. PI says this is the only score meant for talent decisions. |
| Percentile ranks | Where the score sits in a distribution | For context only |
| Raw score and sub-scores | Number correct, number attempted, and number correct in verbal, numerical and abstract | No. PI says these give no meaningful information for decisions. |

PI is firm on sub-scores. It says a 12-minute test cannot show whether someone is stronger in verbal, numerical or abstract reasoning, partly because differences may just reflect which items a person skipped or guessed. It calls using the numerical sub-score to hire accountants a misuse of the assessment.

## Job targets and match scores

Employers set a cognitive **job target** for each role, usually with PI's Job Assessment tool. Your scaled score is compared with that target as a **match score**:

- If your score is **at or above** the target, the match score is 10. PI says there is no evidence that scoring above the target harms job performance, so there is no penalty for being above it.
- If your score is **below** the target, points come off on a sliding scale.

PI says match scores are for ranking and prioritising candidates, not for making a decision on their own. It also tells employers to consider low scores with care. People with a lower cognitive fit for a role may learn at a steadier pace or lean more on experience.

## What counts as a good score

There is no single answer, because each employer sets its own target for each role. PI does not publish typical targets by job.

What PI does publish:

- The general population average is 250.
- Your score is compared with the target for the role you applied for, not with a pass mark.
- Being above the target earns no extra credit, and being below it lowers the match on a sliding scale.

You will see "good score" numbers quoted online. Treat those with caution: they are not from PI, and the only number that matters is the target your employer set.

Our study guidance, not a PI standard: aim to finish practice tests with most of the 50 items attempted and a high hit rate on the ones you answer. If you are reaching 35 to 40 items with few slips on well-made timed practice, you are in good shape to show your real ability.

## Will I see my score?

Often not. PI recommends that employers keep cognitive scores confidential and talk about results in terms of fit ("a moderate fit to the job") rather than raw numbers. PI also says it does not release individuals' questions or their answers. If you want feedback, ask the employer politely. They decide what to share.

## Practice scores do not predict your real score

PI says its official sample questions have not been statistically equated with the real test, and that performance on them "is not a valid indicator of cognitive ability or performance." The same is true of any practice test, including the items in this repo. Use practice to learn the formats and the pace, not to forecast a number.
